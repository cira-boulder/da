Real-time RRFS-Smoke Hybrid Data Assimilation of VIIRS/GOES-16/17 AOD and Surface PM2.5 Observations for Improved Regional Smoke and Fire Weather Forecasting

Funding AgencyNOAA Project NumberNA23OAR4590111 Project PIsHuiying Luo, Blake Allen, Liaofan Lin, Anton Kliewer Performance Period2022–2026

Description:

In recent years, a large increase in the extent of wildfire smoke has occurred across North America, focused on the western regions, but often extending across much of the continental U.S. and adjacent regions. The significant amount of aerosols released into the atmosphere by the wildfires combined with the large areal extent of the wildfire smoke has caused significant adverse economic and health-related effects. The areal extent and severity of localized smoke conditions is highly variable and strongly dependent on the evolution of mesoscale weather features. Because of this, accurate prediction of these smoky conditions, which is highly desirable for providing health guidance information to millions of citizens (as well as for other applications), requires a sophisticated numerical model prediction system and an associated advanced data assimilation system. Work in this project will focus on the data assimilation portion of this combined system, specifically on evolving the GSI-based Rapid Refresh Forecast System (RRFS) version 1 capabilities to a more advanced JEDI-based capability for the RRFSv2 system. These data assimilation capabilities for constraining the model-cycled smoke evolution with satellite smoke Aerosol Optical Depth (AOD) observations, complement other system components, namely the model smoke prediction capability and the fire-radiative-power (FRP)-based active wildfire smoke “injection” algorithm. The work on these other system components is ongoing within our laboratory, led by a collaborator on this proposal, and the work under this proposal will be closely coupled and coordinated with that effort, increasing the likelihood of this proposal leading to a successful transition of this enhanced AOD smoke assimilation capability into operations with the RRFSv2 implementation. Our ongoing research has demonstrated improved aerosol depictions in the model forecast with the assimilation of AOD, a measure of how much direct sunlight is prevented from reaching the ground by aerosol particles. Previous studies have shown a similar benefit from utilizing surface observations of PM2.5, atmospheric particulate matter having a diameter of less than 2.5 micrometers. Assimilating observed AOD and PM2.5 into the RRFS will enable more accurate prediction of weather variables such as temperature, winds, and moisture.

The outcome of this research will be a mature JEDI-based software package for assimilating smoke-filtered AOD products from NESDIS and surface PM2.5 into the RRFSv2 model. As a result of the work in this project, a real-time, online system will be available for user-accessible output and verification and the package will be at RL 8, ready for transition to NOAA operations as part of the RRFSv2 system. The work undertaken here targets the FW-1 priorities regarding transport and evolution of smoke and consequent meteorological impacts via data assimilation and will improve forecasts of smoke-related aerosols, leading to improved air quality forecasts (as well as visibility and other weather elements), benefitting millions of citizens during wildfire events.

CIRA Research of/for Bipartisan Infrastructure Law: Improving Approaches to Extreme Precipitation Estimation for Infrastructure Hazard Risk Planning

Funding AgencyNOAA Project NumberNA25OARX405C0034-T1-01 Project PILiaofan Lin Performance Period2025–2027

Description:

Accurate estimation of the potential “upper limit” for extreme precipitation is critical for dam safety, high-hazard infrastructure planning, and water resources management. Methods used to estimate the theoretical upper limit of precipitation are often outdated and in need of improvement. The Bipartisan Infrastructure Law (BIL) supports efforts to modernize probable maximum precipitation (PMP) estimation, led by NOAA PSL in collaboration with a diverse team of experts. In 2024 NOAA commissioned a study by the National Academies of Science, Engineering, and Medicine (NASEM), “Modernizing Probable Maximum Precipitation Estimation” (DOI: https://doi.org/10.17226/27460). One major component of the study’s recommended approach is to estimate PMP by performing several simulations using a state-of-the-science, physics-based weather model, and then use machine learning approaches to correct for systematic errors in precipitation fields. To achieve this goal, it is first necessary to develop and test a weather model that can meet the needs of estimating PMP as well as develop the necessary postprocessing to correct systematic errors. CIRA, in collaboration with the partners listed above, will work towards developing and testing such a weather model. Specific activities will include:

  • Improving the initial state of the weather model through a process known as data assimilation. Better data assimilation will allow researchers to simulate past extreme precipitation events, which will be used to assess weather model performance.
  • Developing a machine learning tool to correct systematic errors after a weather model forecast is created.
  • Testing different weather model configurations.
  • Developing tools to compare weather model output to past extreme precipitation events. This will inform which weather model configuration is best and what deficiencies still exist that need to be corrected with postprocessing

These efforts are an important step towards realizing the recommendations of the NASEM report, which will result in better PMP estimates. These PMP estimates will then be used in the management and design of dams and other high-hazard infrastructure to ensure that these facilities are safe. Additional information about the BIL PMP modernization project can be found on NOAA PSL’s website: https://psl.noaa.gov/precip/pmp/.

Disaster Relief Supplemental Appropriations: Assimilation of Aerosol Optical Depth (AOD) Smoke and Dust Products in the Rapid Refresh Forecast System

Funding AgencyNOAA Project NumberNA23OAR4050134D Project PIsHuiying Luo, Liaofan Lin, Anton Kliewer Performance Period2023–2026

Description:

Project FIRE 3 will improve the detection and forecasts of the conditions leading to the onset of fires, fire emissions after the onset of fires, and local weather forecasts in areas surrounding wildfires. It is well documented that wildfire emissions are strongly modulated by surrounding weather conditions; aerosols and particulates produced by a fire can also influence and change the state of the surrounding weather. To improve the accuracy of forecasts provided by the next-generation Rapid Refresh Forecast System, CIRA researchers will implement an assimilation procedure to improve initial distributions of aerosol tracers which play a role in wildfire weather feedback. In addition to smoke-flagged aerosol measurements, dust products will also be assimilated to improve forecast skill. Through the interactions of modeled aerosol tracers with radiation and microphysics, the assimilation of satellite-based aerosol optical depth (AOD) smoke and dust products and near-surface fine particulate matter (PM 2.5) measurements will enable more accurate prediction of weather variables such as temperature, winds, and moisture.

CIRA Research of/for Bipartisan Infrastructure Law: Refining Numerical Approaches to Extreme Precipitation Estimation for Infrastructure Hazard Risk Planning

Funding AgencyNOAA Project NumbersNA23OAR4050419I
NA24OARX405C0012-T1-01 Project PILiaofan Lin Performance Period2023–2026

Description:

Accurate estimation of the potential “upper limit” for extreme precipitation is critical for dam safety and water resources management, as dam failures pose significant risks to life and property. Methods used to estimate the theoretical upper limit of precipitation are often outdated and in need of updating. The rarity of extreme events means that old storms with limited observational data are often used to define the upper bound of precipitation. Observations of many important old storms are limited in spatial and temporal coverage, and sometimes of dubious quality. This reduces confidence in flood hazard assessments used in dam safety evaluations and leads to unknown or uncertain societal risk.

The potential effects of anthropogenic (human-caused) climate change are also not generally reflected in the estimates of probable maximum precipitation (PMP) and other metrics of extreme precipitation that are being currently applied to the design and regulation of dams and other infrastructure in the U.S. The procedures used to produce these estimates effectively assume climatic stationarity. There is a solid chain of evidence, however, that the climate at global to local scales has become non-stationary due to anthropogenic influences, with the main change being a pronounced warming trend. There is also increasing evidence that this anthropogenic climate change is affecting, and will increasingly affect in the future, key aspects of storm environments and characteristics, potentially impacting PMP.

A large community of water resources stakeholders, especially dam safety officials and engineers, have a need for updated extreme precipitation estimating tools for design of safe water infrastructure. A comprehensive exploration of newer datasets and potential scientific methods is a necessary first step to update these outdated methodologies and develop 21st century tools that use modern best practices and incorporate the consideration of climate change.

The proposed work will address two main tasks:

  1. Assessing operational NOAA forecast and analysis products and evaluating their potential application for updated PMP estimation methods
  2. Testing and prototyping state-of-the-science numerical weather prediction methods for improved data and the inclusion of climate change considerations

GSL work will concentrate in the second task area, in particular focusing on data assimilation enhancements, ensemble post-processing, and investigation of dynamic core configurations.
